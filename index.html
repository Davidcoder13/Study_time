The Types of Industry
① Primary
Extracting Raw Resources

Directly taking natural resources from the earth or sea. No processing — raw materials only.

  • Farming / agriculture
  • Fishing and aquaculture
  • Logging / forestry
  • Mining (gold, coal, diamonds)
  • Oil and natural gas extraction
  • Trapping and hunting

Processing & Manufacturing

Taking raw materials from the primary sector and turning them into finished goods.

  • Car manufacturing
  • Food processing (canning fish)
  • Lumber mills / sawmills
  • Oil refining
  • Steel production
  • Construction

Services

Providing services to people rather than making physical goods. Largest sector in developed countries.

  • Healthcare and hospitals
  • Education / teaching
  • Retail and restaurants
  • Banking and finance
  • Transportation

Impacts of Resource Extraction — The P3 Framework
Every resource extraction project creates impacts across three dimensions: People (social/human impacts), Planet (environmental impacts), and Profit (economic impacts). You need to be able to evaluate any resource using P3.
👥 People
  • Jobs created in local communities
  • Health risks from pollution or toxic exposure
  • Displacement of Indigenous or local communities
  • Infrastructure built (roads, schools, hospitals)
  • Safety hazards for workers
  • Cultural impacts on traditional ways of life
  • Access to food, water, livelihoods
🌍 Planet
  • Habitat destruction and deforestation
  • Water and air pollution
  • Soil degradation and erosion
  • Loss of biodiversity (species decline)
  • Carbon emissions / climate change
  • Oil spills and toxic runoff
  • Long-term ecosystem damage
💰 Profit
  • Revenue for companies and governments (taxes)
  • Export income for Canada
  • Economic spin-off industries
  • Cost of cleanup and environmental remediation
  • Cost of healthcare for affected communities
  • Long-term vs. short-term economic thinking
  • Community royalties or compensation
Case Study: The Fishing Industry
Overview
  • Fishing is a primary industry — extracting fish from the ocean or freshwater
  • Canada has one of the world's longest coastlines → rich fishing grounds
  • Major areas: Grand Banks (Atlantic cod), Pacific salmon, lobster (Nova Scotia)
  • Employs thousands in coastal communities; part of cultural identity
  • Fish processing (canning, freezing) is a secondary industry linked to it
  • Over-fishing led to the collapse of the Atlantic cod fishery (1992) — a major case study in resource mismanagement
P3 — Fishing Impacts
  • People (+): Provides jobs and food; supports Indigenous fishing rights and traditions
  • People (−): Collapse of cod destroyed thousands of jobs and devastated coastal communities
  • Planet (−): Over-fishing depletes populations; bycatch (unintended catch) kills non-target species; habitat damage from trawling
  • Planet (+): Sustainable quotas and marine protected areas (MPAs) help stocks recover
  • Profit (+): Profitable industry; export value; tourism (sport fishing)
  • Profit (−): Moratorium on cod (1992) cost $400M+ in unemployment benefits
Renewable vs. Non-Renewable Resources
Type Definition Examples Key Issue
Renewable Can be replenished naturally within a human timescale if managed well Solar, wind, hydro, forests, fish, crops Can become non-renewable if overused (e.g. overfishing, deforestation)
Non-Renewable Formed over millions of years; once used, cannot be replaced on a human timescale Oil, natural gas, coal, minerals, nuclear (uranium) Finite supply; extraction causes significant environmental damage
Flow Resources Continuously available — exist regardless of whether we use them Solar energy, wind, tidal/wave energy Cannot be stored easily; location-dependent
Instream Resources Used directly as they flow — must be used where they occur Hydroelectric (flowing water), tidal power Dams affect fish migration and downstream ecosystems
Key idea: Renewable does NOT mean unlimited. Fish are renewable — but the 1992 cod collapse showed that even renewable resources can be destroyed by over-extraction. Sustainability requires using resources at or below the rate they can naturally replenish.
Q: What is the difference between primary, secondary, and tertiary industries? Give one example of each.
Primary: Directly extracting raw natural resources from the earth. Example: mining, logging, fishing.

Secondary: Taking raw materials and processing or manufacturing them into finished goods. Example: a sawmill turning logs into lumber, or a cannery processing fish.

Tertiary: Providing services rather than producing goods. Example: teaching, healthcare, banking, retail stores. In developed countries like Canada, most people work in the tertiary sector.
Q: Using the P3 framework, explain one positive and one negative impact of the Canadian fishing industry on the Planet.
Negative (Planet): Overfishing depletes fish populations. The collapse of the Atlantic cod fishery in 1992 is a key example — decades of intensive fishing removed cod faster than they could reproduce, causing a near-total population collapse. Bottom trawling also destroys seafloor habitats and creates significant bycatch (unintended species caught and killed).

Positive (Planet): Fishing quotas, moratoriums (bans), and Marine Protected Areas (MPAs) can allow fish stocks to recover over time. When managed sustainably, the fishing industry can harvest food without permanently damaging marine ecosystems.
Q: What is the difference between a renewable and a non-renewable resource? Can a renewable resource ever "run out"?
A renewable resource can replenish itself naturally within a human timescale (e.g. fish, trees, solar energy). A non-renewable resource was formed over millions of years and cannot be replaced once used (e.g. oil, coal, natural gas).

Yes — a renewable resource CAN effectively run out if we use it faster than it can replenish itself. The Atlantic cod collapse is the classic example: cod are biologically renewable (they reproduce), but decades of overfishing removed them faster than they could breed, causing a functional collapse. This is why sustainability — using resources at a rate nature can sustain — is so important.

Oil Sands & Pipelines
// what are they · key issues · P3 impacts
What Are the Oil Sands?
  • Located mainly in northern Alberta (Athabasca Oil Sands)
  • A mixture of sand, water, clay, and a thick, heavy oil called bitumen
  • Canada has the 3rd largest oil reserves in the world — mostly in the oil sands
  • Bitumen must be extracted then upgraded / refined before use
  • Two extraction methods:
    • Open-pit mining: strip away the boreal forest and dig up the sand — used when oil is near the surface
    • In-situ (SAGD): inject steam deep underground to loosen bitumen so it flows up — less surface disturbance
  • Requires massive amounts of water and energy to process
What Are Pipelines?
  • Underground pipes used to transport oil, bitumen, and natural gas across long distances
  • Canada has one of the world's largest pipeline networks
  • Key pipelines: Trans Mountain (Alberta → BC coast), Keystone XL (controversial — cancelled), Line 5
  • Pipelines are more efficient and lower-emission than tanker trucks or rail for transport
  • But pipeline leaks and spills are a major environmental and political concern
  • Building pipelines requires crossing through Indigenous territories — major rights issue
Key Issues with Oil Sands & Pipelines
Environmental Issues
  • Oil sands produce 3× more GHG emissions per barrel than conventional oil — major climate impact
  • Massive tailings ponds — toxic wastewater lakes that can leak into groundwater and rivers
  • Boreal forest destruction: thousands of km² of forest cleared for open-pit mining
  • Water use: extracting 1 barrel of oil requires 2–4 barrels of water
  • Pipeline spills contaminate rivers, soil, and drinking water
  • Habitat destruction affects woodland caribou, migratory birds, and other species
Political & Social Issues
  • Western alienation: Alberta argues Ontario and Québec block pipelines while benefiting from equalization payments funded by oil revenue
  • Pipeline routes cross Indigenous territories without proper consent — violation of UNDRIP
  • Federal vs. provincial jurisdiction: Who controls resources? Who approves pipelines?
  • Keystone XL: US cancelled it citing climate concerns — lost billions in Canadian investment
  • Jobs debate: thousands of high-paying jobs vs. long-term environmental damage
  • Energy security: Canada relies on US markets — no east-west pipeline, so no way to sell oil to Europe or Asia easily
Oil Sands P3 Analysis
👥 People
  • POSITIVE
  • High-paying jobs (avg. $100K+/yr in Fort McMurray)
  • Economic booms for Alberta communities
  • Government revenue funds public services
  • NEGATIVE
  • Displacement of Indigenous communities
  • Health concerns: cancer clusters near tailings ponds (e.g. Fort Chipewyan)
  • Boom-bust cycle: workers lose jobs when oil prices crash
  • Mental health issues tied to "fly-in/fly-out" work culture
🌍 Planet
  • POSITIVE
  • Reclamation efforts: companies required to restore mined land
  • In-situ (SAGD) method reduces surface disturbance
  • NEGATIVE
  • Massive GHG emissions — Canada's single largest source
  • Tailings ponds toxic to wildlife (bird deaths)
  • Boreal forest loss — critical carbon sink destroyed
  • Water contamination downstream (Athabasca River)
  • Reclamation is slow and incomplete
💰 Profit
  • POSITIVE
  • Canada earns billions in oil export revenue
  • Alberta Heritage Trust Fund (oil savings)
  • Federal royalties and corporate taxes
  • NEGATIVE
  • High extraction costs — only profitable at high oil prices
  • Cleanup and reclamation costs are enormous
  • Long-term liability: taxpayers may pay for abandoned sites
  • Stranded asset risk if world moves to clean energy
Q: What are the oil sands, and why are they environmentally controversial?
The oil sands are deposits of bitumen (heavy crude oil) mixed with sand, water, and clay, found mainly in northern Alberta. They are controversial because extraction is highly destructive: open-pit mining requires clearing massive areas of boreal forest, creating toxic tailings ponds that can leach into groundwater, consuming enormous amounts of water, and producing approximately three times more greenhouse gas emissions per barrel than conventional oil. The Athabasca River downstream has shown signs of contamination, and Indigenous communities nearby (like Fort Chipewyan) have reported elevated cancer rates. While profitable, the environmental costs are enormous and long-lasting.
Q: Identify one "People" positive and one "Planet" negative impact of oil sands development, using P3.
People (Positive): The oil sands create tens of thousands of high-paying jobs in Fort McMurray and across Alberta. Workers in the oil sands earn well above average Canadian wages, and the industry generates billions in government revenue that funds public services like healthcare and education for all Canadians.

Planet (Negative): Oil sands operations are Canada's single largest source of greenhouse gas emissions, contributing significantly to climate change. Additionally, massive toxic tailings ponds covering hundreds of square kilometres risk leaking into the Athabasca River system, harming aquatic life and potentially contaminating drinking water for downstream communities, including Indigenous communities like the Mikisew Cree and Athabasca Chipewyan First Nations.

Indigenous Territorial & Resource Rights
// rights framework · oil sands impacts · Grassy Narrows case study
Indigenous Resource Rights
  • Indigenous peoples have treaty rights that guarantee access to land, hunting, fishing, and resources
  • Section 35 of the Constitution Act (1982) recognizes and affirms Indigenous rights
  • Canada is a signatory to UNDRIP (UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples) — requires Free, Prior, and Informed Consent (FPIC) before development on Indigenous lands
  • The government has a Duty to Consult Indigenous communities before approving resource projects on or near their territories
  • Many First Nations argue consultation is insufficient — they want veto power over projects on their land
  • Land claims and treaty negotiations are ongoing across Canada
Impacts of Oil Sands on Indigenous Communities
  • Oil sands sit within or near Treaty 6, 7, and 8 territories — First Nations signed these treaties believing their rights to the land would be protected
  • Boreal forest clearing destroys hunting, trapping, and fishing grounds
  • Water contamination in the Athabasca River — communities downstream report contaminated fish and drinking water
  • Fort Chipewyan (Athabasca Chipewyan and Mikisew Cree First Nations): reported elevated cancer rates; government initially dismissed concerns
  • Tailings ponds have leaked, contaminating land used for traditional practices
  • Many communities were not meaningfully consulted before pipelines and oil sands projects were approved on their territories
  • Economic benefit has been unequal — some communities negotiated impact-benefit agreements, many received little
🐟 Case Study: Grassy Narrows First Nation

BACKGROUND

  • · Located in northwestern Ontario near the English-Wabigoon River
  • · From the 1960s–1970s, a paper mill (Reed Paper Co.) in Dryden discharged mercury-laden wastewater into the Wabigoon River
  • · Mercury travelled downstream and contaminated fish in Grassy Narrows' territory
  • · Fish were the primary food source and cultural cornerstone for the Anishinaabe community
  • · Residents consumed contaminated fish for years before the extent was known

IMPACTS (P3)

  • ·People: Widespread mercury poisoning (Minamata disease) — neurological damage, tremors, vision loss, birth defects. Community still affected today. Loss of traditional fishing culture and food security.
  • ·Planet: River ecosystem severely damaged. Mercury bioaccumulates up the food chain — fish, eagles, humans. Contamination persists decades later.
  • ·Profit: Tourists and sport fishers stopped coming. Traditional economy destroyed. Government eventually paid compensation, but decades too late. Cleanup costs enormous.
  • ·Outcome: Grassy Narrows fought for decades and won a landmark Supreme Court ruling (2014) giving them control over logging in their territory. Still fighting for full river cleanup.
Q: What happened at Grassy Narrows, and what does this case study teach us about resource development and Indigenous rights?
A paper mill in Dryden, Ontario discharged mercury into the English-Wabigoon River from the 1960s–70s, contaminating fish that were the primary food source for the Grassy Narrows Anishinaabe community. Residents ate the contaminated fish for years, leading to widespread mercury poisoning (Minamata disease) — causing neurological damage, tremors, blindness, and birth defects that still affect the community today.

This case teaches us that: (1) Industrial development near Indigenous communities can cause irreversible harm to both people and the environment. (2) The government failed to protect an Indigenous community's right to clean water and traditional food. (3) Meaningful consultation and consent before development could have prevented this. (4) Environmental impacts are not felt equally — Indigenous and lower-income communities are disproportionately harmed. The community's decades-long legal fight also shows the strength of Indigenous resistance and the importance of rights recognition.
Q: What is the "Duty to Consult," and why is it often controversial when it comes to pipeline approvals?
The Duty to Consult is Canada's legal obligation (grounded in Section 35 of the Constitution and confirmed by the Supreme Court) to meaningfully consult with Indigenous communities before approving any project that might affect their treaty rights or traditional lands.

It is controversial because: (1) Many First Nations argue that "consultation" falls short of true consent — the government talks to them but can still approve projects over their objections. (2) UNDRIP requires "Free, Prior, and Informed Consent" (FPIC), meaning Indigenous peoples should have a genuine say (and arguably a veto). (3) Pipeline routes often cross unceded (never-surrendered) territories, where Indigenous title was never legally extinguished. Groups like the Wet'suwet'en Nation (who opposed the Coastal GasLink pipeline) argued they never ceded their land and cannot be forced to accept pipelines through it.

Ecological Footprints
// definition · productive land types · Canadian footprint · carrying capacity · sustainability graph
What Is an Ecological Footprint?
An ecological footprint measures the total amount of biologically productive land and water needed to produce the resources a person (or country) consumes AND absorb the waste they generate. It is expressed in global hectares (gha).
What Makes Up Your Footprint?
  • Food: What you eat — meat requires far more land than vegetables
  • Housing: Size of home, energy used to heat/cool it, materials used to build it
  • Transportation: Car use, flights, public transit — burning fuel leaves a carbon footprint
  • Goods & Services: Everything you buy required land, energy, and materials to produce
  • Waste: How much garbage you produce — landfills use land; decomposing waste produces methane
  • Carbon: The land needed to absorb your CO₂ emissions (forests act as carbon sinks)
Canada's Ecological Footprint
  • Canada has one of the largest ecological footprints per person in the world (~8–9 gha/person)
  • The global average is ~2.7 gha/person
  • The fair Earth share (what's actually available per person on Earth) ≈ 1.6 gha/person
  • This means Canadians use about 5× their fair share of Earth's resources
  • Key drivers: cold climate (high heating needs), car-dependent cities, long-distance food systems, high meat consumption, energy-intensive industry
  • Canada's large land area means it has a biocapacity surplus — but globally, humanity is in overshoot
Types of Productive Land
🌾
Cropland
Land used to grow food, animal feed, and biofuels. Most productive and most in demand.
🐄
Grazing Land
Pasture used for raising livestock (cattle, sheep). Meat has a very large land footprint.
🌲
Forest Land
Land needed to absorb CO₂ (carbon sequestration) and produce timber and paper products.
🐟
Fishing Grounds
Ocean and freshwater areas needed to sustain seafood consumed. Includes marine ecosystems.
🏗️
Built-Up Land
Land covered by cities, roads, buildings, and infrastructure. Permanently removed from natural use.
⚡
Carbon Land
Forest land needed to absorb CO₂ from burning fossil fuels. Often the largest part of footprint.
Key Sustainability Terms
Carrying Capacity

The maximum population that an ecosystem (or the planet) can sustainably support with the available resources without degrading the environment.

Fair Earth Share

The amount of biologically productive land available per person on Earth if it were divided equally. Currently ≈ 1.6 gha per person. Canadians use ~8–9 gha — far above their fair share.

Biocapacity

The total amount of biologically productive land and water an area (country or planet) has available to regenerate resources and absorb waste.

Overshoot

When humanity's ecological footprint exceeds Earth's biocapacity — we are consuming resources faster than they can be replenished. We are currently in global overshoot every year (Earth Overshoot Day gets earlier each year).

Ecological Deficit (LOAD)

When a country's footprint exceeds its own biocapacity — it is a "debtor" nation, borrowing from other countries' resources or from future generations.

Sustainability

Using resources at a rate at which they can naturally replenish, so that future generations can meet their own needs. The goal is to live within Earth's carrying capacity.

The Sustainability Graph — Understanding Overshoot
The sustainability graph shows two lines over time: Ecological Footprint (demand — what we use) and Biocapacity (supply — what Earth can provide). When demand exceeds supply, we enter overshoot.

Reading the Graph

  • ·Green line (Footprint): rising — humanity uses more and more resources each year
  • ·Blue line (Biocapacity): roughly flat or declining — Earth's productive land is not growing (and is being lost to urbanization/degradation)
  • ·Yellow dot (Crossover ~1970s): the point when humanity first exceeded biocapacity
  • ·Red zone (Overshoot): the gap between demand and supply — we are in "ecological debt"

Consequences of Overshoot

  • ·Fish stocks collapse (overfishing)
  • ·Forests shrink (deforestation)
  • ·Freshwater aquifers depleted
  • ·CO₂ accumulates → climate change accelerates
  • ·Soil degradation reduces agricultural capacity
  • ·Biodiversity loss — species extinction
Q: What is an ecological footprint? Why is Canada's footprint considered unsustainable globally?
An ecological footprint measures the total amount of biologically productive land needed to support a person's or country's consumption and absorb their waste. It's measured in global hectares (gha).

Canada's footprint is approximately 8–9 gha per person, one of the world's highest. The global fair Earth share — how much land is actually available per person if divided equally among all humans — is only about 1.6 gha per person. This means Canadians consume roughly 5× their fair share of Earth's resources. If everyone lived like Canadians, we would need approximately 5 Earths to sustain humanity. This is unsustainable because it means other countries have less available, and we are drawing down resources faster than they can replenish — contributing to global overshoot.
Q: Define carrying capacity, overshoot, and fair Earth share. How are they related?
Carrying Capacity: The maximum population an environment can support indefinitely without degrading it. Earth has a carrying capacity — a limit to how many people it can sustain at current consumption levels.

Fair Earth Share: If all of Earth's biologically productive land (~13.1 billion gha) were divided equally among all humans (~8 billion), each person would get about 1.6 gha — this is the fair Earth share. It represents what's actually available per person.

Overshoot: When humanity's total ecological footprint exceeds Earth's total biocapacity. We are currently in overshoot — meaning we are consuming more than Earth can regenerate in a year.

Connection: When our demand (footprint) exceeds the planet's carrying capacity (biocapacity), we go into overshoot. The fair Earth share shows what each person's "budget" would be if we lived sustainably within Earth's limits. Canadians far exceed this budget, contributing to global overshoot.
Q: On the sustainability graph, what does the red "overshoot" zone represent? What happens if it keeps growing?
The red overshoot zone represents the gap between humanity's ecological footprint (demand) and Earth's biocapacity (supply). When the footprint line rises above the biocapacity line, we are using more resources than Earth can regenerate — we are drawing down natural capital (like spending from savings rather than living within your income).

If it keeps growing, the consequences include: fish stocks collapsing (overfishing beyond recovery), forests shrinking, freshwater aquifers drying up, more CO₂ in the atmosphere accelerating climate change, and ultimately a forced reduction in the human population's standard of living as resources become scarce. The key message is that overshoot is not indefinitely sustainable — at some point, nature's ability to regenerate will be so degraded that it cannot support current population levels.
